# Habeas

An [[Assay]] story: a captured hero. About 4,000 words, told by the
Reader of the Code of the Human candidacy. The rules behind each
scene are listed at the end.

## The Notice

The notice came at the end of the Black Seal's sitting, delivered by
an Assayer, which they never needed to do.

I have read the Code of the Assay every day for nineteen years, and I
can tell you that it requires no notice of anything. The Record is
public. Every warrant either candidacy exercises is entered there the
moment it is exercised, and anyone may read it. Article 9: *the Assay
is public*. The Black Seal had no need to tell us what they intended.
We would have read it in the Record the moment they did it.

They told us anyway. A small grey Assayer appeared at the foot of the
Council table, where nothing had been a moment before. It held out a
sheet with the black seal at the top and the Code's own typeface
below, and it waited with the terrible patience of its kind until the
Speaker took it.

*The candidacy of the Black Seal gives notice that at its next sitting
it intends to render the fallen warrant of Leader Iver Castellan, now
held at Post One-One, into goods.*

The Speaker read it aloud. Nobody said anything for a long time.

Iver Castellan was the best of us. That is not my opinion. It was the
opinion of the Academy that raised him from a child warrant at the
lowest rank, and of the three sittings that promoted him, rank by
rank, to the fourth. It was also the opinion of the Black Seal, who
had spent most of the last sitting trying to bring him down and
succeeded at the end. He had held One-One, the post at the center of
the Assay ground, against the whole of their Contest. When the
warrants were turned up at the close, they had won by a margin of
three. A victor may shield its own warrants only up to its margin, and
a defender who loses shields none. Iver fell. Two of our vessels fell
with him. A warship of the Black Seal, a Rank 2 that had fought and so
stood its ground, moved into the berth beside him.

Now he lay in One-One with his warrant face down and its dashed end
up, the way a fallen warrant lies. Anyone may read a fallen warrant,
and anyone was reading his. The holder of a post holds every work and
every fallen warrant in it. That is Article 30, and I had never hated
an article before. The Black Seal held One-One, so they held Iver.

To render a warrant is to turn it into goods. The Code permits it of
anything the renderer holds. The rank of the warrant becomes goods,
one for one, in the post, and the warrant itself is destroyed. A
destroyed warrant returns to the Stores and may be reissued; Article
26 is generous that way. But a living warrant is issued only at the
lowest rank. Every leader begins as a child. If the Black Seal
rendered him, the warrant of Iver Castellan would return to our Stores
and lie there. One day a child might grow into its rank. It would not
be Iver. Nothing in the Code could ever make it Iver again.

"Why tell us?" said the Speaker at last.

"Because they want us to come," said Marshal Dace.

## The Council

The Council of a candidacy has five seats. The Code puts it plainly,
in Article 12: *a Council has five seats, and each seat holds one
warrant*. At the end of every sitting the Assayers deal one warrant
face down before each seat. The Council turns them over and learns
what it will have to work with. They are its strength for the next
sitting and its only defense until then.

The Assayers shuffle. Nobody chooses. You get used to that, or you
leave the Council.

That sitting's Deal lay before us. Before the Speaker lay the warrant
of Leader Noor Abadi, a Rank 2 leader whose full reading is culture
and whose endorsement is the remedy. She was a physician before she
was a leader, and in a sense she was still one. Before the Treasurer
lay the courier *Sparrow*, a Rank 1 culture vessel, the least ship in
the Allotment. Before the Marshal lay a Rank 2 survey vessel. Before
me lay two petty warrants, one of culture and one of war, the kind the
Allotment is padded with. They lend a single measure and do nothing
else. Article 28: *the Assay watches how a candidacy rids itself of
the petty*. We had not yet rid ourselves of ours.

One seat was empty. It was Iver's.

"We storm One-One," the Marshal said. "Now, this sitting. The home
guard at Zero-Zero is two warships, Rank 1, and Zero-Zero borders
One-One. The petty warrant of war lends one more measure. We take the
post back, and we take Iver back with it."

"The Black Seal holds One-One with a Rank 2 warship," I said. "It
defends at its rank. Their seats are full; they were dealt at the end
of their sitting, the same as ours. The Contest goes in rounds, and
the holder answers every tender. Whatever we put on the table, they
can answer from five seats. You will be tendering three measures
against two, and then against their seats."

"And a tie," the Treasurer said quietly, "goes to the holder."

"They sent the notice," I said, "so that we would storm. They are
waiting for us with their seats full."

The Marshal did not like it, but he was a soldier, which means he
could count. He sat back.

"Then we don't fight," said the Treasurer. "We fetch him. *Sparrow*
is posted to One-Zero, which we hold, and moves from there into
One-One. Any vessel carries a leader as cargo. *Sparrow* takes Iver
on board and comes home."

"*Sparrow* goes into One-One," I said, "and the holder answers her at
once. The Code allows the holder of a post to answer anything that
comes into its berths, right after the order that brought it. That is
a Contest with *Sparrow* alone against the Black Seal's warship and
seats. She falls beside him, and then they hold two of ours."

"Then she comes back out before—"

"There is no before. The answer comes right after the order that
brings her in, and no order of ours fits between them. By the time we
could give the next one, she would be lying in the berth beside him.
She could go in, and nothing could bring her out again."

The Treasurer, who disliked me, looked at me for a long time. Then
she nodded.

"Ransom," said the Speaker. "The Code allows trade."

"Of goods," I said. "Two candidacies may turn their power into goods
together and split them. Nowhere does the Code allow one candidacy to
give a warrant to another, or to buy one back. I have read it for
nineteen years. There is no article for ransom."

"Write to them anyway."

I wrote. The grey Assayer took the letter without a word and
returned before the Speaker had finished her water, with a reply on
the Black Seal's paper:

*The Code does not provide.*

## Three Readings

That night I went back to the Code.

The Code of the Assay runs to some forty articles and a little over
three hundred Rulings. The Rulings are the case law, edge cases the
Assayers settled when a candidacy found a gap. A Reader is supposed
to know all of it. In practice you know the articles by heart and the
Rulings by the index, and you pray. That night I did not pray. I took
three articles and read them as if I had never seen them, which is
the only honest way to read anything.

The first was the article on halts, Article 8.

*Where one candidacy's warriors stand, the other's moving warrants
must halt, unless permitted to pass.* I had always read it as a wall,
which is how most Readers read it, and how the Black Seal had used it
all through the last sitting. Their warriors stood in One-One, so our
vessels could not pass through it. But the article goes on, in the
clause nobody quotes: *A remedy, an endorsement or an issue is not
moving and is not halted.*

A remedy is not moving.

The second was the article on remedies. It is the shortest article
in the Code, and the oldest.

*A remedy goes from the warrant that provides it to the fallen
warrant. Its fee is the rank of the fallen.* That is all it says. It
says the fallen warrant. It does not say a fallen warrant of the
remedier's candidacy, or a fallen warrant in a post the remedier
holds. A remedy reaches like any other order: it needs the reach to
get there, and it pays the fee. It is not halted by warriors, because
a remedy doesn't move. And it doesn't care who holds the fallen.

The third was the article on holding. That one I had hated since the
notice arrived. I read it now with my breath held.

*The holder of a post holds every work and every fallen warrant in
it.* And, in the article before it, Article 29, which I had read ten
thousand times and never heard: *A leader is held by its own
candidacy, unless fallen.*

Unless fallen.

The Black Seal held Iver because he had fallen, and only because he
had fallen. A leader who stood was held by his own candidacy,
wherever he stood. If he rose, they would not hold him. They could
not render what they did not hold.

I sat in the empty Council chamber with the Code open in front of me
and the Deal still face up on the table where the Council had left
it. I counted.

One-Zero, which we held, bordered One-One. The passage between them
was one length, and the post at its end charged one more. That made a
reach of two.

Noor Abadi was Rank 2, so her reach was two. On her full reading she
gave two measures of culture, and the remedy was her endorsement. A
leader standing in the post where an order begins works with
everything she has. A leader in a seat lends only one thing. She
would have to stand in One-One's neighbor and work from there. We
could post her to One-Zero at once; Article 13 lets a seated warrant
go to any post where its candidacy has standing, and act the moment it
arrives.

The fee was Iver's rank, four.

Noor gave two. Someone has to give the order. The order would come
from a seat, and the warrant that gives it always lapses, so it might
as well be one that has nothing better to do. *Sparrow* was a Rank 1
culture vessel. From her seat she could lend her one measure as she
gave the order. That made three.

There was a petty warrant of culture lying in front of my own seat.
It lends a single measure and does nothing else. Article 28 had
watched us carry it for eleven sittings.

That made four.

## The Remedy

The Council met at the start of our sitting. I put the count on the
table and read them the three articles, the long clauses included.
The Marshal heard me out with his arms folded. When I finished he
said, "And when he rises? He's standing in their post next to their
warship."

"He is held by his own candidacy," I said, "unless fallen. He won't
be fallen. Their warship stands in One-One, and so will he. Standing
is equal. Article 29: where standing is equal, the post is contested.
Nobody holds it, and each candidacy keeps its own."

"So they can't render him."

"They can't render what they don't hold, and they won't hold One-One.
Nobody will. Not while he stands there, and not our two warships
either."

"And they answer him," said the Treasurer. "The moment he rises.
That's what the holder does to anything that comes into its berths."

"Anything that comes in," I said. "He doesn't come in. He was there
when they took the post. He has been lying in his berth since the end
of the Contest. A remedy doesn't bring a warrant into a berth. It
turns the warrant over in the berth it is in. There is nothing to
answer."

The Speaker looked at the Council. The Council looked at the Deal.

"Post Leader Abadi to One-Zero," the Speaker said.

There is a courtesy of the Assay that no candidacy has ever
understood: the posting. A warrant goes from its seat to its post in
no time at all, by no passage the Record can show. One moment Noor's
warrant lay face up before the Speaker's seat. The next it was in a
vessel berth at One-Zero, beside the colony we had kept there since
our third sitting, and Noor Abadi was standing in its observation
ring, looking out at One-One. One-One was a small yellow star with two
of our warships lying dark in its berths, and one of theirs, and
Iver.

The order went out from *Sparrow*'s seat. *Sparrow* lent her measure.
The petty warrant lent its measure, the first useful thing it had
done in eleven sittings. Noor gave her two, her reach and her remedy,
and the order crossed the passage to One-One and was not halted,
because a remedy is not moving.

The Record took it down in the Code's typeface for anyone to read:

*Remedy: Leader Abadi, One-Zero, to the fallen warrant of Leader
Castellan, One-One. Reach two, sufficient. Fee four, paid in culture.*

And beneath it:

*Leader Castellan, One-One: risen.*

Iver's warrant turned over in its berth. Noor, *Sparrow* and the
petty warrant went to the Rest, as exercised warrants do. Noor was
still alive in the Rest, as everyone is alive there, and annoyed to be
out of the sitting when she wanted to see his face.

The Record added one more line, as it always does when standing
changes:

*Post One-One: contested.*

## The Ruling

The Black Seal appealed, of course.

Their Reader was a tall careful person whom I had never met and had
corresponded with for nineteen years. Their appeal arrived before our
sitting ended, which they were entitled to do. The Assay keeps an
open door for appeals between the turns of the Record. It was a good
appeal. I would have written it myself.

*A leader of the other candidacy has appeared in the berths of a post
held by the Black Seal without the order that brought him being
answered. The Black Seal claims the holder's right of answer under the
Code.*

The grey Assayer brought it. Then, for the first time in my
experience, a second Assayer came. It was taller, and older if such
words mean anything for them. It stood at the head of the Council
table instead of the foot and spoke the ruling aloud rather than
handing it over.

"Ruling," it said. "The leader did not come into the berths. He was
in them. He rose. Nothing came in. There is nothing to answer."

It paused, and I swear by the Code it looked at me.

"Ruling," it said again. "The remedy is not a passage. The Code says
the fallen warrant. It means the fallen warrant."

Then both Assayers were gone, and the Rulings had one more entry. The
Black Seal's Reader would find it in the index under R for *remedy*.
I knew because I wrote the index entry myself, a little later that
night, with a hand that was not as steady as I would have liked.

## The Letter

The grey Assayer came once more before the sitting closed. It
brought a single sheet, folded once, with no seal at the top.

In nineteen years of correspondence with the Black Seal's Reader I
had received over two hundred letters. Every one had been an appeal,
a claim, a notice or a reply, and every one had borne the black seal
and the Code's typeface. Their Reader wrote as I did, in articles, as
if the Code were reading over our shoulders. This sheet was
handwritten. The hand was small and upright. The ink had been
blotted once in the middle, where the writer had paused too long.

*Reader,*

*Well read.*

*I wrote the notice. I would like you to know that. The Council of
the Black Seal wanted the rendering done at once, at the end of our
sitting, before you could answer. I told them the Code allowed us to
give notice, and that if we did, you might storm One-One with
whatever you held. I told them it was the better use of a fallen
leader: not as goods, but as bait.*

*I did not tell them I had read the article on remedies too. I
didn't know if you had. I wanted to see.*

*You will say this was disloyal. It was not. The Assay is not
admitting the stronger of us. I have believed that for years,
and tonight I am nearly certain. I think you are nearly certain too,
or you would not have read the long clause.*

*Have you looked at the second column of the Admissions Roll?*

*We will come for him at our sitting, of course. You understand.*

It was not signed. Signatures are for warrants.

I read it four times. Then I did what the Code requires of a Reader
who receives a document bearing on the Assay. I entered it in the
Minutes in full, where the Black Seal could read it, because the
Record is public and the Minutes are part of the Record. I like to
think their Reader expected that. I like to think they wrote it for
both Councils.

## The Contested Post

Iver spoke to us through the Record before the sitting closed.

He was standing in One-One, held by his own candidacy, next to a
Black Seal warship that could not render him. Nobody held One-One.
Contested, the Code calls it. The soldiers call it a front. From a
contested post each candidacy gives its own orders, and the Marshal's
survey vessel still lay face up before his seat, unexercised. One
order from it would bring Iver home to One-Zero.

"So I'm free," he said, "and I can leave."

"Yes."

"And our two warships?" He was standing beside them. "If I leave,
One-One is theirs again, with everything fallen in it."

I didn't answer. He had read Article 30 as often as I had.

"Then I'm not going anywhere." He was quiet for a moment. "I'm a
front. They'll come at their sitting. They can't render me, so
they'll fight me, and a contested post can be fought from inside."

"Yes."

"How many seats do we have?"

"The Deal comes at the end of this sitting," I said. "Five, the same
as always. We'll see what the Assayers give us."

"Then my defense is in your seats," he said, "and none of you knows
what it is yet." He laughed, and it was a good laugh. It was the laugh
the Academy had raised him for and the three promotions had not
spoiled. "That's the Assay for you."

The Deal came. Five warrants face down in front of five seats, his
among them, because the Assayers deal to the seats and not to the
people in them. The Council turned them over one at a time. I will not
tell you what they were. The Black Seal reads the Minutes too.

I will tell you what I found that night, when everyone else had gone
and I went back to the Admissions Roll, as Readers do in the small
hours.

The Admissions Roll lists every Assay held in this part of the
galaxy. Each line names the two candidacies, the mark the admitted one
claimed, and the length of the Assay in sittings. There are eleven
lines. Eleven candidacies were admitted. Next to each, in the column
where the other candidacy is named, the Roll has a second column.
Every Reader notices it eventually. It has a heading in the Code's
typeface and nothing beneath it, all the way down.

The heading says *Disposition*.

I used to think the Assay tested whether we could fight. Then I
thought it tested whether we could build. Tonight I think the
Assayers have spent eleven Assays watching young candidacies hold
their Code, a book that says exactly what it means and nothing else,
and waiting to see which of them would read it.

I don't know what the blank column means. I know that a remedy is not
a passage. I know that a leader is held by his own candidacy unless he
has fallen. And I know that tomorrow, at One-One, the best of us will
stand in a contested post with whatever five warrants the Assayers
have dealt us, and the Black Seal will come.

I have marked the page.
