# The Held Chord

An [[Overtone]] story: a great battle at the end of the game. About
4,000 words, sung by a later singer of the Reed. Each part opens with
a verse of the Hymnal. The rules behind each scene are listed at the
end.

## I. The Listening

> *Count the held and not the struck,*
> *the struck note falls, the held note stays.*
>
> — The Hymnal, verse 39

This is how we sing it to the children, and this is how it was.

At the end of the long war between the Reed and the Bell, there came
a verse when the Reed held the Full Chord.

You know the Full Chord. Every child of the Reed knows it before she
knows her own name. In each of the four voices a people sings, there
is one highest note. It is the eighth harmonic, the last that holds;
above it sound breaks into noise. The Low voice has its eighth, and
so do the Hearth, the Forge and the High. When a people holds all four
eighths at once, at the moment of the Listening, it has sung the Full
Chord. Its song has reached the end of what a song can be.

But the Hymnal says *held*. It does not say *sung*. Every child asks
why, and the answer is the hardest thing in the Hymnal. A note that
is struck falls. When a hearth or an instrument sings, it drops to its
low note, its weaker harmonic, and stays there until the tuners
retune it. When a cantor or a horn sings, it goes to rest, and a
voice at rest is not in the chord. The Full Chord can't be sung, only
held. You keep your four highest notes unsounded at the moment of the
Listening, with everything around them burning.

In that verse the Reed held four.

The Great Horn held the Low eighth. It was the largest war instrument
our people ever made, so large that its song bent space around a
whole star. It lay in the star called Lumen and had not sounded in
four verses.

The Hearth Organ held the Hearth eighth, in the star called Hollow.
It was a choir and a pipe at once, raised and built in a single
verse eleven verses before. The Forge Organ held the Forge eighth, in
the star called Anvil.

And the cantor Ione held the High eighth.

There was no greater voice among the Reed. She had been raised at the
fundamental, the first note, as every living voice is raised. No voice
is born above the first note. She had grown one step at a time, every
step paid for in hearth-song at a Choir School, up through every
harmonic to the eighth. She stood in Lumen beside the Great Horn. Her
high note was the High eighth, the highest anyone had ever heard. Her
low note, which she had not turned to in a hundred verses, was the
Low seventh, a war voice.

That was the danger of that verse, and the children should hear it.

The Bell had gathered at Cinder. Cinder was their star, and it lay
next to the star we called the Narrows. Everything sings and every
song is heard, so there was no secret in it. The whole sky could hear
the Bell horns tuning in Cinder. They were the same horns as ours. The
Bell sing the same intervals we do, because the octave is two to one
under every sun. Only their timbre differs: they ring where we
breathe. Four war voices of the Bell stood in Cinder, three horns and
a cantor, in four of its five war places. The Narrows was all that
stood between them and the inner stars.

The Narrows was the only way in. Around the inner stars, on every
side but one, lay Silence: lanes that carry no song, where nothing
can pass. Only the Navigators had ever sung across Silence, and the
Bell had no Navigator left. Every song from Cinder to Lumen, to
Hollow or to Anvil had to pass through the Narrows. In the Narrows
stood old Tamas, a war cantor of the fourth harmonic, with two war
horns, the *Vigil* and the *Kestrel*. War voices in a star catch a
passing song. The Hymnal says so in verse 8, and Tamas had held the
Narrows for nine verses on that verse alone. No Bell song could pass
through it while he stood there. A song could end there, but then it
would meet him.

The war council of the Reed met at the beginning of that verse, and
the war council wanted to strike.

"Cinder is full," said the cantors of the Low voice. "Three horns and
a cantor. They will come at the Narrows. Let the Great Horn go to
Cinder first and silence them where they stand."

Ione said nothing, because it was not her voice that should answer.
The old tuner Sabel answered. She kept the Organs and knew the cost
of everything.

"If the Great Horn sings in Cinder, it contests. A horn that contests
keeps its place. That is true, it doesn't go to rest. But at the end
of a contest the victor shields only as much as its margin. The Great
Horn is the eighth harmonic. To keep it from being silenced we would
have to win in Cinder by eight. Can you win by eight against three
horns and a cantor, and whatever they hold in breath?"

Nobody could.

"Then the Great Horn falls silent in Cinder," said Sabel, "and there
is no Low eighth, and no chord."

"Then let Ione go," someone said. They said it softly, because it was
a terrible thing to say. "Let her turn over to her low note and go to
the Narrows as a war voice of the seventh. A war voice of the seventh
stands like a wall. She would hold the Narrows alone."

That was true. A war voice in a contest stands at its harmonic, and
the seventh is a great harmonic. Ione turned over, at the Narrows,
would be a wall. And there would be no High eighth, because a turned
voice sounds its other note, and the chord would break as surely as
if the Bell had broken it.

Then Ione spoke.

"I will hold," she said.

So the Reed held. Nobody struck Cinder. Nobody turned. The Great Horn
lay silent in Lumen with Ione beside it, the two Organs stood in
their stars, and the Reed sang only small songs that verse, songs of
travelling and placing. They sang them with small voices, so that
everything that sounded went to rest and the four great notes stayed
held.

One of the small songs was a hush. In the Narrows stood a tower of
the Low voice that carried the Hush, the only art that can silence a
song standing in a star. Sabel sang out two small voices from breath
into the last two war places of the Narrows: the *Wren*, a war horn of
the first, the least horn of the Reed, and a rattle of the Low voice,
a single beat. The tower hushed them as they came, and paid for it by
falling to its low note. From then on the sky heard five war voices in
the Narrows: Tamas, the *Vigil*, the *Kestrel*, and two hushed ones
that could have been anything.

Then came the Listening. All the sky listens at the end of a people's
verse, to hear what that people holds. It heard the Low eighth in
Lumen, the Hearth eighth in Hollow, the Forge eighth in Anvil and the
High eighth in Lumen, held and unsounded.

The Full Chord.

And because the Reed had reached the end of a song, the Coda began.

## II. The Coda

> *When one song reaches its end,*
> *each other song sings one verse more.*
>
> — The Hymnal, verse 38

The Coda is the last law of the sky, and it is a fair one. When a
people reaches the end of its song, it doesn't win at once. Every
other people sings one last verse. In that verse they can break the
chord, or reach an end of their own, or fall silent trying. When
their verse ends, the sky listens one more time, and whatever is held
then is what is held forever.

The Bell had one verse. If they broke any of our four notes in it, by
silencing the Great Horn, or Ione, or an Organ, or by taking a star so
that an Organ sounded for them instead of us, the Coda would end with
no chord.

Before the Coda, though, the Reed had to draw breath.

You know the breath too. At the end of every verse a people draws
breath: five voices from the Waiting Choir. Nobody chooses which. The
Waiting Choir rises in an order that no one chooses, and that is the
oldest unfairness and the oldest fairness of the sky. Those five are
the people's breath for the verse to come. They can sing out in any
star that hears the people's song, or lend a note to a song anywhere.
And they are held through the other peoples' verses. Whatever a
people spends in defense during its rivals' verse is breath it won't
have in its own.

The Reed drew breath at the end of the verse of the chord, and this
time there would be no verse of our own to spend it on. The whole
breath was for the Narrows.

Sabel read it out to the war council. The *Thorn*, a war horn of the
sixth harmonic, the best horn the Waiting Choir had given us in ten
verses. A hearth horn of the second and a forge horn of the second,
neither of them war voices. A hearth horn of the first, the *Linnet*.
And a child cantor of the High voice named Pell, raised only nine
verses before, at the fundamental like every voice.

"Five," said Sabel. "Four of them can only lend a note. The *Thorn*
lends a wall."

Then the tuners retuned. The Hymnal allows three tunings at the end
of a verse, and the Organs had not sung, so they needed none. Sabel
retuned the Hush-tower at the Narrows, fallen to its low note for the
hush, and two hearths in Lumen, and the verse of the Reed was over.

The Coda began. It was the Bell's verse.

## III. The Narrows

> *War voices catch a passing song:*
> *it ends with them, or not at all.*
>
> — The Hymnal, verse 8

The Bell began by singing out.

A voice in breath can sing out in any star that hears its people's
song, and Cinder heard the Bell's. Their cantor Hask sang out one of
the Bell's five breaths into Cinder, a horn, into the last free war
place. A voice that sings out can sing in the same verse, and a voice
that stands in the star where a song begins sings with everything it
has. A voice lending from breath gives only one note, and it gives it
openly: breath is unheard, but a note lent from breath is heard by
all. Only the Hush hides a song, and the Hush works only on voices
standing in a star.

Hask kept four breaths. One would begin the song. Three were held,
and nobody but the Bell knew what they were. The whole sky could see
that three were held, though. Breath is unheard, but its count is
not.

Every song needs one voice from breath to begin it, and that voice
always goes to rest afterward. A song of war began in Cinder and went
to the Narrows.

It was the Contest of Songs.

The singers say Hask chose well. In a contest the side that begins it
names the voices of the ground that must answer, and Hask named all
five war voices of the Narrows, the hushed ones too. A war voice
answers at its harmonic. Tamas was the fourth, the *Vigil* the fourth,
the *Kestrel* the second: ten that the sky could hear. And two hushed
voices that nobody outside the Reed could weigh.

The Bell's horns rang from Cinder with their war-song, and the whole
sky heard that too: fifteen.

Every child of the Reed knew what the held breaths meant, and so did
every cantor in the war council. If the Bell won the Narrows, their
horns that came through the Contest unsilenced would move into it,
and the Narrows would be theirs. One held breath was all it would
take to begin a second song from there, into Hollow, or Anvil, or
Lumen. An Organ is not a war voice. It answers a contest with a
single note.

A song that is answered is weighed only at the end, when every voice
has fallen quiet. Until then each side adds voices in turn. The
ground adds first. That is the old courtesy of the Contest.

## IV. The Contest of Songs

> *No die is cast and no lot drawn:*
> *you sing, you hush, you hold, you fall.*
>
> — The Hymnal, verse 41

Here the children always lean forward, because here is the battle.

The Reed added the *Thorn* from breath. A war horn of the sixth
answers at the sixth, and the sky heard the Narrows ring with it.
Ten became sixteen against the Bell's fifteen, and two hushed voices
besides. Sabel wanted the Bell to hear that: *if you stop now, you
lose.*

The Bell answered. Hask sent a song from another star, from the horns
the Bell kept in Ember, behind Cinder. A song sent to help another
song isn't caught on the way, and the war-song in Cinder swelled to
twenty.

The Reed added the Hush-tower itself. It stood in the Narrows,
retuned to its high note, a tower of the Low voice of the fourth, and
a war voice answers at its harmonic. Twenty against twenty, and two
hushed voices nobody could weigh.

Hask listened for a long time. The singers say the whole sky
listened with them. Two hushed voices in the Narrows could be
anything. They could be two rattles, or two horns of the sixth that
the Bell had last heard of ten verses ago and lost track of.

Then the Bell answered with one of their three held breaths: a war
cantor of the fourth, Hask's own sister. With her came the charge the
Bell had been holding, a held breath of power that lends any voice,
up to what the voice can carry, and she could carry a great deal.
Twenty-six. And Hask kept the other two breaths.

The Reed added everything that was left in breath: Pell, the
*Linnet* and the two horns of the second. None of them was a war
voice, and a voice that isn't of the Low answers with a single note,
the least note, whatever its harmonic. Four notes. Twenty-four that
the sky could hear, and the two hushed.

Hask kept the two breaths and fell quiet.

That was the moment of the Narrows, and the war council of the Reed
saw it clearly. The Reed knew what the hushed voices were, so they
knew the sum: twenty-six against twenty-six, and a tie goes to the
ground. But the Bell still held two breaths, kept for the second
song, and whatever they were, the Bell could still add them after the
ground had added its last.

The Reed had nothing left in breath. And in Lumen, two lengths behind
the Narrows, Ione stood beside the Great Horn and listened.

She was the High eighth, and in that verse that was all she was. No
cantor can turn over in another people's verse. She could not become
the Low seventh, the war voice, the wall. She could do one thing. A
song sent to help another song is not caught on the way, and from
Lumen she could lend the Narrows one note, the least note, as every
voice not of the Low does. One note would make a margin of one, and a
victor shields as much as its margin. It would shield the *Wren*.

The singers say Tamas felt her listening. Tamas spoke to her across
the two lengths of lane, because a song can be heard anywhere, and
the Hymnal keeps what he said:

*If you sing, the chord is lost whatever the weighing says. If you
hold, it is lost only if they outsing us and their held breaths get
through. Hold.*

And Ione held.

The Reed fell quiet. Hask, holding two breaths that the Reed feared
and could not see, fell quiet too. Nobody in the Reed knew then that
both were hearth voices, no war voices at all, and that the Bell's
charge was spent: they could not have added one note to a war-song.
When every voice has fallen quiet, the Contest is weighed.

The hushed voices were unhushed, and the sky heard what they were:
the *Wren*, a single note, and the rattle, a single beat. The terror
of the Narrows had been made of the two smallest things the Reed
owned.

The songs were weighed.

The war-song of the Bell: twenty-six.

The answer of the Narrows: Tamas, the *Vigil* and the *Kestrel*, ten.
The *Thorn*, six. The Hush-tower, four. Pell, the *Linnet* and the two
horns of the second, one each. The *Wren* and the rattle, one each.
Twenty-six.

Twenty-six against twenty-six.

A tie in the Contest goes to the ground.

## V. The Held Note

> *The ground keeps what it holds*
> *on an even song.*
>
> — The Hymnal, verse 41, the old reading

This is the part the children don't like, and you must sing it to
them anyway.

The Reed won the Contest of the Narrows, and the Narrows fell silent.

A victor shields only as much as its margin, and on an even song the
margin is nothing. The ground keeps its star, but it shields no one.
Every voice that stood in the Narrows and answered the Contest was
silenced: Tamas, the *Vigil*, the *Kestrel*, the *Wren*, the rattle
and the Hush-tower. And every horn of the Bell that had sung from
Cinder was silenced in Cinder. The voices that had lent a note, from
breath or from Ember, went to rest, as voices that lend do. The child
Pell was among them, alive in the Rest, as everyone is there.

So nobody stood in the Narrows any more but a forge tower of the
Reed that the Bell had not named, and a tower catches nothing. Tamas
lay silenced, and a silenced war voice catches nothing either. For the
first time in ten verses any song could pass the Narrows to Lumen,
Hollow or Anvil.

And no song came.

Hask still held two breaths. But a song of war must be led by a war
voice standing where it begins or where it ends. A voice from breath
can only lend. Every Bell war voice within reach was silenced in
Cinder, or at rest after lending from Ember. The Bell had horns left
in the sky, standing and unsilenced, but they were far away, and no
song could carry them in time. Hask could not even sing the two
breaths out into Cinder: its five war places were full of silenced
horns, and a silenced horn keeps its place until it is broken or
healed. At the drawing the two breaths sank into the Rest unsung,
because no one can hold a breath across a drawing.

The Coda was a single verse. It ended.

The sky listened one last time.

The Low eighth was in Lumen: the Great Horn, held, unsounded through
the whole war. The Hearth eighth in Hollow and the Forge eighth in
Anvil were both at their high notes, untouched, tuned. The High eighth
was in Lumen too: Ione, unturned and unsounded, who had heard her
people's last wall fall two lengths away and had not sung.

The Full Chord.

That is how the Reed ended its song.

And now, children, the part you have been waiting for. Once the Coda
was over and the chord had been listened to and held, there was no
law left that could break it. Ione sang.

She sang her high note, the High eighth, for the first time in a
hundred verses. The Great Horn sounded with her, and the two Organs in
Hollow and Anvil, all four eighths together, struck at last and held
at the same time. Everyone says that can't be done, and it can be
done exactly once, at the end of everything. It was the sound of a
song that has reached its end.

Everything sings, and every song is heard by everyone. The Bell heard
it in Cinder, where their horns lay silenced in every war place, and
the singers say Hask wept, because the Bell sing the same intervals we
do, and it was their chord too. The Drone heard it in the wild stars,
where it had hummed over every new people since before the first
fundamental. For one verse, the old singers say, the
Drone fell silent to listen.

The chord is still sounding. Go outside on a clear night and listen
between the stars, where the dark carries song and no rest, and you
will hear four notes held.

Tamas lay silenced in the Narrows the whole time. He had held it for
ten verses with three war voices, and for the last verse with the
two smallest things we owned. He never heard the chord with his own
ears, and he was its first note.

That is why we sing the hold before we sing the chord.
